# Searching for the Perfect Adventure

First things first, let’s get this out the way - there’s no perfect Table Top Role Playing Game (TTRPG) out there.  Certainly there’s no pinnacle of game that’s designed so well and comprehensive that it’s the end-all-be-all game for everyone (or almost everyone) out there.  There’s the saying: “When you design for everyone, you design for no one.”  So, I don’t want to waste time searching for something I know doesn’t exist.  Every game is going to have some quirks that some people do and don’t like.  That’s totally fine!  If anything, I’m excited to see some odd rules and bizarre systems.

So, if you decide to journey with me, let’s define what it is we’re searching for, and why have we decided to embark on this epic quest?  As for my part, I think we should find something that has fast paced combat, where your players aren’t overwhelmed with decisions every round.  It should let role playing situations play out in mechanically meaningful ways.  It should have depth and flexibility.  It should onboard new players with ease and keep veteran players engaged.

Ideally, this unicorn of a system should let fighting with a melee weapon be interesting every round (I hate the repeated rounds of “I guess I hit it with my axe.”), as well as not make magic users feel like they have to save spell usage until “the right moment.”  

Small Tangent: In regards to spell casting limitations, I’d equate it to something like health potions.  It’s a common feeling for a player to grab every health potion they see and never use them because they “might need them later.”  This results in health potion hoarding.  I find mechanics like the Dark Souls Estus Flask solution to health potions a very interesting one.  Having a couple known triggers to refilling a resource makes using it feel less risky, even if there’s a cost to doing so.  You know you’ll get more health potions when you do X or Y, so use it now because it means nothing to keep it forever.  While I can’t speak for all players, magic use can fall victim to the same issue.

Now, how are we going to find this treasure?  This gem of a system?  We’re going to read about them.  We’re going to play them.  One by one, we’ll discuss what was good and what was less good about various TTRPG systems.

Also, I’m not an expert in this, so don’t get your hopes up.  I’m no game design aficionado.  Most of my experience in TTRPGs comes from D&D 5e, some Pathfinder 1e, and Cairn (a game inspired by Into the Odd, which you may know).  I’m familiar with others, such as Mork Borg, Kids on Bikes, Knave, Dungeon Crawl Classics, and others, but I haven’t played any of those (as of the time of writing).

If nothing else, I hope this is an interesting journey.  I hope it helps us all find something we’d like to try with friends.  I hope, perhaps most of all, that it’ll help me justify spending an ungodly amount of money on TTRPG game books.

This is not meant to be a review blog.  I don’t intend on recommending or not recommending any particular system (feel free to quote me on this when I inevitably do exactly this).  All I want to do is talk about aspects of different TTRPGs that work for me and don’t.

If anyone reads this and has recommendations for a system I need to play, please let me know!  I’m open to anything - from high quality, fully featured hardback books down to 8.5” x 11” paper folded into a pamphlet with rules printed on it.

So, let’s delve into the depths of ancient and bespoke TTRPG caverns and see what treasures we can find, and what dragons we can awaken!  Happy adventuring!


## Cairn
Cairn is a well constructed system created by Yochai Gal, and inspired by games such as Into the Odd and Knave.  I haven't played any of the games that inspired Cairn, so I won't be comparing it to the systems that came before it.  As such, I have no idea what aspects are borrowed from other systems, or completely unique to Cairn.  

The game focuses on narrative first gameplay, facilitated by a Game Master role that they call the Warden.  Its mechanics support interesting story (and character) progression.  In truth, I adore this system and think it's a great way to introduce new players into TTRPGs.  Not to say that Cairn is "baby's first TTRPG" by any means, but it has a way of stripping down the heavy and cumbersome rules that plague some systems.  The mechanics are interesting, and random, enough that I think veterans of the genre would stay engaged throughout a longer campaign.   

I cannot understate how much I like the simple stats and d20, roll under, system Cairn is based on.  Tracking down all manner of numbers and modifiers to add or subtract from their roll on an ability check can be tedious.  In Cairn, your abilities are tied to one of three scores: Strength, Dexterity, or Willpower.  The rulebook has examples of what types of skills can be tied to each ability score, but overall I think the game has a "do what feels right" kind of vibe.

The best thing about Cairn comes from a section in the rulebook for Wardens when talking about difficulty:

Is what the player describes and how they leverage the situation sensible? Let it happen.

This is incredible.  This gives so much power to the Warden to help facilitate a rewarding player experience.  If you've played a TTRPG, you know how frustrating it is to have even your most basic of plans go to hell because of a bad dice roll.  If the maneuver a player is attempting is risky, or has high stakes, then yes - of course - a die roll should come into play and build that tension.  However, if it makes sense, or would be more interesting for the narrative if something succeeded, you "let it happen."  

When a die roll is needed, you make a "save."  For example: if you need to roll a Dexterity save in order to avoid being seen by the bandits outside their lair, you would roll a d20 and see if the number on the die is equal to, or less than, your Dexterity score.  If yes, you succeed!  If not, you fail.  This is intuitive and is easy to explain to new players.

As for combat, in Cairn, attacks always hit - you just roll damage.  The damage die you roll is the damage you deal, no bells and whistles to track down and add/subtract anything when attacking.  This keeps everything straightforward and makes combat feel tight, which is one of Cairn's greatest strengths. 

In order to thwart incoming damage, characters have a single armor value.  This armor is pure damage reduction, which continues that feeling of well paced combat.  You take the number your attacker dealt to you, subtract your armor value, and apply that to your person.  

There is a limit on the total armor a player can have, which I'm not a huge fan of.  I'd like to see players given the opportunity to be a hulked out, steel plated tank - but if they choose that route, they pay a cost somewhere else.  There's no concept of advantage/disadvantage when making saving throws, but if there were, it might be worth it to impose disadvantage on Dexterity saves if you're over a certain armor threshold.

I don't take much issue with the armor system, truth be told.  It's just something I might have designed differently; not that I would have done better (definitely) or worse (hopefully), just different. 

There are mechanics around groups of attackers in Cairn that I think are an interesting answer to the issue of Action Economy, where each individual character would normally get their own unique attack during combat.

If multiple attackers have the same target, you roll each of their damage dice, but only apply the highest roll.  This is true not just for monsters attacking players, but for players attacking the same monster, as well.  This makes lopsided fights more feasible in the favor of the underdog.  It's not guaranteed of course, but it's a step in the right direction.  It also has the added benefit of making a mistake not feel like it's massively detrimental during a fight.

One thing I think works well with the inventory system is how Cairn uses Fatigue.  If you gain a point of Fatigue, it's marked on your inventory, effectively "occupying" a slot.  Inventory management is a core part of Cairn, and I like how this creates the idea that the more tired you are, the fewer items you can carry.

While Cairn isn't perfect, I struggled to pull a whole lot from it that didn't work for me.  One thing I'm not keen on is how the inventory system is indirectly tied to casting spells.  This takes a bit of an explanation.

In Cairn, your inventory is limited to 10 slots.  The rulebook breaks it down to say that 6 slots are your backpack, and 4 slots are meant for your worn armor and weapons.  Most items take up a single slot, but some (like plate mail, 2 handed swords, etc.) have the Bulky attribute and take up 2 slots.  In contrast, smaller items (like chalk, chisels, and glue) can take up a single slot at the Warden's discretion.  

So, what does any of that have to do with spellcasting?  Using magic, which any character can do, is also tied to Fatigue.  Thematically, this makes sense.  Your character is conjuring arcane powers using their sheer force of will.  This would be exhausting!  Since Fatigue is tied to your inventory, this means casting spells effectively takes up inventory space, which is awesome how it intrinsically encourages spellcasters to not wear big bulky armor, and conversely, armor wearers not to put much emphasis on spells.  

What's not great about this is how limited it makes casting magic, even if you're stripped to your clothes and a few rations.  Spellcasting requires either a Spellbook, Scroll, or Relic.  Spellbooks can only contain one spell, and must be held in both hands in order to cast.  Scrolls don't cause Fatigue when cast, but are single use items.  Relics, which are probably the most powerful of the three, don't cause Fatigue, but have a limited number of charges.  However, most Relics have a recharge condition, making them indefinitely useful.

I'd like something that encourages spellcasting a little more.  Not endless spellcasting, of course, but where being a wizard meant you felt like a proper wizard.  Players can take time to improve the effectiveness of spells, but otherwise there's no progression of magic.  You're likely to feel no more powerful as a mage after 50 sessions than you did after 5.

Cairn is a solid TTRPG system that does a lot of things right.  If you're looking for something that's rules lite and leans into a narrative focused experience, this could be the game for you.  Also, it's free - which is never a downside.

While the inventory and spellcasting mechanics don't suit my tastes, they are concise systems that work together in interesting ways.  They definitely serve the type of game Cairn is, and I can't fault it for that.
